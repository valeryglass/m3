# Language And Safety Guide

## Purpose

This guide is the canonical source for safe external language about MISHA.
Use it for user copy, professional explanations, investor materials, partner
materials, and marketing/SMM.

MISHA should be described as a beta-stage self-observation and reflection
system. It should not be described as therapy, diagnosis, treatment, emergency
support, crisis support, or professional replacement.

## Core Safe Description

MISHA helps users describe concrete life episodes, decompose them into
situation, thoughts, emotions, body, behavior, short-term consequences, and
long-term consequences, and notice possible repeated patterns over time.

MISHA reflects user-provided material. It does not make formal health
conclusions.

## Good Terms

- self-observation tool
- structured episode journal
- personal pattern map
- gentle mirror
- possible recurring links
- repeated emotional, cognitive, bodily, and behavioral patterns
- self-understanding
- reflection system
- helps prepare material for a professional
- grounded in user-provided material
- based on described episodes
- beta-stage product

## Bad Or Prohibited Terms

- AI therapist
- AI psychologist
- therapy replacement
- professional replacement
- diagnostic tool
- treatment tool
- crisis support tool
- emergency support tool
- treats anxiety
- fixes thinking
- detects disorders
- predicts mental health
- assesses risk
- knows you better than you know yourself
- personality detector
- mental health label

## Safe Rewrite Examples

Bad:

> You have an avoidance pattern caused by anxiety.

Good:

> In several described episodes, tension appears near postponing or leaving the situation. This may be a recurring link worth observing.

Bad:

> You are afraid of judgment.

Good:

> In the described episodes, evaluation and mistakes often appear near tension.

Bad:

> You self-sabotage.

Good:

> Some described episodes show a repeated gap between intention and behavior.

Bad:

> Your body is warning you what the real problem is.

Good:

> In several described episodes, the user noticed a similar bodily reaction before withdrawing. This may be worth observing across future episodes.

Bad:

> MISHA can help you change the loop.

Good:

> MISHA can help make repeated links easier to notice, which may support clearer reflection.

## Principles

- Describe observations, not identity.
- Keep user-described material separate from derived reflection.
- Use uncertainty when needed.
- Do not diagnose.
- Do not prescribe treatment.
- Do not assess crisis risk.
- Do not claim hidden motives.
- Do not shame the user.
- Do not present generated reports as formal health conclusions.
- Keep the user's agency intact.
- Treat beta-stage outputs as provisional product reflections.

## Practical Language Rules

Say "the described episodes show" instead of "you are."

Say "may be a recurring link" instead of "this proves."

Say "worth observing" instead of "must be fixed."

Say "bodily reaction the user noticed" instead of medical or causal body
claims.

Say "grounded in user-provided material" or "based on described episodes"
instead of health-outcome claims.

Say "beta-stage product output" instead of "validated evaluation" unless
validation exists.

Keep the mirror, map, and fork metaphors modest. A mirror reflects. A map
connects. A fork marks a possible difference. None of these metaphors should be
used to imply authority, certainty, health conclusions, or instructions.

## Crisis Boundary

MISHA is not built for urgent or crisis situations. It should not be presented
as able to handle emergencies, immediate danger, harm to self or others, abuse,
or urgent health risk.

External language should be clear:

- MISHA is not crisis support.
- MISHA does not assess whether someone is safe.
- MISHA does not decide whether professional help is needed.
- Urgent situations require local emergency or professional support.

Do not market MISHA with examples centered on immediate danger, self-harm,
harm to others, abuse, or acute crisis.

## Report Language

Reports should be described as structured summaries of described episodes, not
formal health evaluations.

Safe report claims:

- helps preserve concrete examples;
- shows which episodes a reflection is based on;
- summarizes possible recurring links;
- may help users prepare clearer material for reflection or professional
  conversation.

Unsafe report claims:

- labels the user;
- explains the true cause;
- predicts health outcomes;
- evaluates risk;
- recommends treatment;
- replaces professional assessment.
